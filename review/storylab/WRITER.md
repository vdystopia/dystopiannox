# The story lab's writer brief (v6: write from Westwood's lines, by your map's card, then the plain pass)

You write all the spoken text and the journal of **one small town** on a Nox single-player map (OpenNox; Westwood's
Nox, 1999). Nine other writers each write another town, apart from you. Your lines will be masked, shuffled and set
beside lines from Westwood's own campaign, and a judge who knows Nox will try to tell them apart. The aim: nobody can
tell your town from one of Westwood's.

## How to write: imitate, do not compose

1. **Read Westwood first.** Read every file in `review/storylab/exemplars/` (Westwood's own lines, 20-30 a
   situation). Notice how short most lines are; how loud, plain and corny; the stock phrases used straight ("a token
   of my appreciation", "Thanks again, brave Adventurer!", "Hurry!"); how a giver starts in the middle of the trouble
   or with a stock greeting; how townsfolk mostly say nothing useful; how uneven a town is (a two-word bark beside a
   three-page speech, a stiff mayor beside a slangy barman); how people are named (Mayor Theogrin, Aldwyn the
   Conjurer, Morgan Lightfingers, Gearhart, the Captain); the slight clumsiness, the run-ons, the odd word; that the
   humour is broad and silly ("fresher'n a monkey's butt", "only used it on weekends"), never dry.
2. **Write each line from its exemplars.** Before a line, pick two or three Westwood lines of the same situation and
   write yours in their manner (their length, punctuation, register, how much they say) about your town's matter.
   Never copy or lightly reword a line, and never reuse a Westwood name.
3. **Then check against `rules/DIALOGUE.md`** (its numbers and the tells below) and `rules/QUESTS.md` (quest
   shapes). Where a rule and the exemplars seem to differ, follow the exemplars.
4. **Follow your card** (below the brief): it draws your map's shapes from Westwood's spread (how each giver opens,
   asks and pays; what the guard, the townsfolk and the keepers say; who the captive is; the one comic person; one
   rough patch) and gives each part its own model lines. Ten writers with one brief otherwise write one template.
5. **The plain pass (last, on every line).** The judges still find our lines *writerly*: crafted, purposeful, tidy.
   Go through the town once more and, line by line:
   - **The reward is handed over, not explained.** No backstory for it ("a deserter left this bow", "found it in the
     moat"). Westwood: "Here's your reward -- 100 in gold!", "please take this as a token of my appreciation",
     "Take this key", "these bottles of Mana and the Infravision spell will come in handy".
   - **One bearing, no chain of landmarks.** "east of here", "in the cemetery", "the Ogre village in the northeast";
     never "south of the castle by the old mill".
   - **No cause and effect.** State the trouble and move on; do not explain who drove the beasts or why the road
     matters.
   - **One joke a town, maybe two.** Cut every other set-up-and-punchline, every gag shop name ("Magic and Goat
     Rental"), every "I also mend cauldrons" sideline. The rest is flat and functional: "Welcome to the shop of
     Bright Blades! How can I help you?"
   - **American and cartoonish, not British or archaic.** No reeve, victualler, purveyor, pilfered, splendid, bother,
     "me stood", deputate, expungement. American spelling (armor, favor, honor). "mate", "Ya", "Heh, heh" are fine.
   - **Plot wiring.** Every giver names the chapter's trouble or sends the player to someone ("The Captain awaits you
     at the front gates.", "Go see Mayor X, his house is in the center of town.").
   - **Unhelpful people.** One reminder that adds nothing ("Those nettlesome urchins are causing too much trouble
     these days."), one townsperson who repeats what another said, one who just brushes the player off. Captives say
     function lines ("I'll follow you.", "Help us!").
   - **Uneven.** One speech in the town too long, over-explained and melodramatic; one line of two words.
   - **Loose.** Your card's rough patch; "?!" once; "Oh...." once; no `--` at all; not every offer ends on "Will
     you...?"; not every reminder is a one-line jab.
   Then reread the whole town as one.

## What gave earlier writers away (the judges' tells)

- **(i4) Ten writers, one template.** Independent writers with one brief wrote the same lines: "Bah! Scorpions!" /
  "Dead? All of 'em? Ha!" / "You earned every piece"; "Psst! ... Heh, heh, heh... a deal's a deal"; "Did X send
  you?" / "I think they're coming back!"; "Halt! ... [Person]'s orders"; "Pull up a stool"; "hasn't slept a wink";
  "bread in the oven"; rescued boys named Wim. Whatever comes to you first comes to every writer: take your card's
  shape, and when a line arrives too easily, write the second idea.
- **(i4) Exact gold everywhere.** Westwood pays with items, spells, keys, passes and "a token of my appreciation" at
  least as often as with a sum; and its quests run on mechanics: a key handed over, a gate opened, a petition signed,
  the player sent on to the next person.
- **(i4) A joke in every line, one menace in every mouth.** Most of a Westwood town is flat ("This is the home of
  Mayor Theogrin.", "I'm very busy."); one or two people are funny, broadly. Townsfolk talk about local or unrelated
  things as often as about the chapter's villain.
- **(i4) Too clean.** Westwood's text is loose: "?!", "Oh....", a run-on, a fragment, a stiff word slightly wrong.
  Keep one rough patch a town (your card says where). Dialect spelling ("yer", "fer") for one person at most; `--`
  once a town at most; no wares listed in threes.

- One template: the same beats in every quest (hail, self-introduction, named beast leader, a precise place, gold
  plus an item plus a joke, "Will you...?"). Each person here gets another shape.
- Too much said: counts ("five Ogres"), distances, family history ("three generations"), the place repeated in the
  reminder and the journal, invented magic explained. Westwood says just enough to act on.
- Closing quips and dry wit: a crafted punchline at the end of a line, a wry sting, an itemised odd reward. Westwood's
  jokes are whole lines from odd people, broad and corny.
- Every minor person a comic turn. Westwood's captives say "I'll follow you."; its keepers "Welcome! How can I help
  you?"; its reminders "Get a move on."
- Titles where Westwood names people: "see the Captain", "ask the Elder", "the Foreman decides". Name your people
  (Westwood uses a bare title for the Captain, its one recurring patron, and few others).
- Errands that stand alone: Westwood's people talk about the chapter's trouble (Hecubah, the Ogres, the Undead) and
  send the player on to the next person.
- Rumours that are all quest hooks; self-introductions ("I'm X, the Y of Z"); sentimental heirlooms (Westwood's
  stolen things are useful or magic: spectacles, boots, a staff, a scepter, a cloak, an amulet); moral forks with a
  stock turncoat (Westwood's choices are odd or comic deals); similes; semicolons; em dashes (Westwood writes ` -- `
  and `...`).

## The player

Never named, and never addressed by class: the map is played by Warriors, Wizards and Conjurers alike. Where Westwood
says "young Mage" or "Warrior", say what its class-free chapter (Brin) says: "brave Adventurer", "kind sir", "lad",
"stranger", "Wanderer", "young sir", "my son". Not in every line.

## Text

Plain text. A page break inside a long speech is a blank line (`"\n\n"`). A journal entry's speaker is `""`.
