# The story lab's writer brief (v14: each quest part from its own Westwood line, the matter yours and plain)

You write the quests and the journal of **one small town** on a Nox single-player map (OpenNox; Westwood's Nox,
1999). Nine other writers each write another town, apart from you. Your lines will be masked, shuffled and set beside
lines from Westwood's own campaign, and a judge who knows Nox will try to tell them apart. The aim: nobody can tell
your town from one of Westwood's, and every line is your own writing, not Westwood's with the nouns changed.

## How to write

1. **Read Westwood first.** Read every file in `review/storylab/exemplars/` (Westwood's own lines, 20-30 a
   situation) for the voice: short, loud, plain and corny, stock phrases used straight, flat functional people beside
   one or two odd ones, American-cartoonish, a little clumsy.
2. **Each part from its own line.** Each part of each quest (the offer, the refusal, the reminder, the thanks,
   afterwards) is dealt one of Westwood's lines of that kind, each from a different Westwood quest. Write the part to
   its line's size and rhythm: the same pages and sentences, about as many words and never more than a tenth over,
   the ! and ? where they fall, how it opens and ends, its register and quirks, its stock phrases. Its sentences become
   sentences about your quest that do the same jobs. Because the parts come from different Westwood quests, your
   quest is no single one of Westwood's; earlier rounds that rewrote one Westwood quest per quest were recognised by
   judges who know Nox, and the lab's originality check measures exactly that.
3. **The matter is your scenario's and nothing more.** Westwood states the trouble, the place and the ask, and stops.
   One trouble, said once and plainly, in the stock terms of the genre (a beast took it, thieves broke in, the Ogres
   carried her off). At most one other person named besides the speaker. No backstory, no logistics (routes, how a
   door opens, who will row you), no local colour or scenery (the town's look is the map's business), no persona or
   running gag, no aside of your own, no closing sentiment, no hint in a reminder that the frame does not have.
   Rewards plainly: gold, a potion, boots, a spell, a key, or "a token of my appreciation".
4. **The other parts from their line frames.** Journal entries, the captive's lines and the townsman take their
   rhythm from the line frame dealt for them. A captive says what Westwood's captives say ("I'll follow you.",
   "Help us!"); a journal entry is one plain order.
5. **Then check:** `rules/DIALOGUE.md` (no semicolons, no em dashes, the player never addressed by class), and the
   originality check your brief names. Fix what it flags in your own words.

## What gave earlier writers away (the judges, i4-i13)

- **Too much in a speech.** Backstory, logistics, named helpers, local trade colour, a deduction, a warning, a gift at
  the ask, a hand-off to someone else, all in one offer (i13, i10). Westwood: the trouble, the place, the ask, stop.
- **A crafted voice.** A sustained persona (cranky, brusque, gossipy), idioms ("I'd wager my last tooth"), dry or wry
  asides, knowing whimsy, quiet grief or understatement, literary turns ("Forgive my tears"), performed mannerisms
  (stammering ellipses, "Um...", "Hmph!"), slips that look placed. Westwood's people are earnest, loud, stock and a
  little stiff; their flaws are naive, their humour broad and rare.
- **One template across writers.** Whatever comes to you first comes to every writer ("Did X send you?", "Heaps and
  heaps of gold?", a pay chest and a missing courier). Follow your own lines, not the obvious idea.
- **Tidy and specific.** Exact sums everywhere, wares in threes, precise landmarks, journals with hints or reasons.
  Westwood's journals are one plain order; its thanks are generic and effusive.
- **Titles and self-introductions.** "I'm X, the Y of Z", "the Reeve", "the Elder". Westwood's people start in their
  trouble and are named by others.
- Semicolons, em dashes, similes, British village words (reeve, moot, victualler), dialect for more than one person.

## The player

Never named, and never addressed by class: the map is played by Warriors, Wizards and Conjurers alike. Where Westwood
says "young Mage" or "Warrior", say what its class-free chapter (Brin) says: "brave Adventurer", "kind sir", "lad",
"stranger", "young sir", "my son". Not in every line.

## Text

Plain text. A page break inside a long speech is a blank line (`"\n\n"`). A journal entry's speaker is `""`.
