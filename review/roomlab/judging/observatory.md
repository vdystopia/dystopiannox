# Observatory: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign rooms of this type, described by their qualities, not room by room.

## What it is for

An astronomer's room, where the sky is watched and charted: a study turned to the stars. Westwood's campaign has no room of this type; its instruments and star charts appear only as odd pieces in other rooms. A sheet may show rooms of the kin work types (a herbalist's room, a kitchen, a laboratory, a library, a smithy or a winch room). Judge each picture as a room of its own type, by its design.

## What you notice first

In an observatory, a telescope or an orrery with room round it. In Westwood's scholarly work rooms, the laboratories and libraries, it is the work along the walls: workstations in a run, or bookcases end to end, sometimes dozens of them.

## What it holds

An observatory would hold its instruments, star charts and zodiacs on the walls, bookcases, a desk and a chart table. Westwood's scholarly rooms hold a desk and chair, bookcases, a table (sometimes with a meal on it), and some ornament: small statues, tapestries, a vase, a painting, a few ornaments among the stacks. Workstations and alchemists' desks belong to the laboratory.

## How it is arranged

In Westwood's laboratories and libraries the pieces keep to the walls, three or four walls used; the middle is open or holds one table with chairs. Libraries are strongly regular, bookcases in long aligned runs; laboratories less so.

## Density and feel

Balanced and scholarly: about a tenth of the floor covered, half to four fifths open, five to fifteen kinds of thing in a large room, fewer in a small one.

## Variants

Westwood's scholars' rooms are mostly in its towns.

## What a designer's hand looks like here

Asymmetry and use: one wall heavily lined, another nearly bare; a meal left on a table beside the work; a single jar or painting; a rug in one room and none in the next. Pieces come in uneven numbers, not matched sets.
