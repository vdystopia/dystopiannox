# Armoury: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign rooms of this type, described by their qualities, not room by room.

Westwood's armouries are few and all small: closets of gear, never halls.

## What it is for

A garrison's arms and armour kept ready to hand: a closet of gear rather than a hall of it.

## What you notice first

A rack or two of arms standing against a back wall: pole-arm racks most often, otherwise an armour stand, a clothes rack or a bow rack.

## What it holds

- Common: two or three racks, mixed in kind; pole arms most often.
- Sometimes: swords hung on a wall, a helm shelf, a chest, a crate or two, a single barrel.
- Not seen: tables, chairs, beds, shelves of goods, sacks, a counter, hangings of décor, plants.

Only three or four kinds of thing and four to six pieces in a room.

## How it is arranged

Everything stands against the walls, mostly the two back walls, and the middle is usually left empty. Where both back walls are used, racks take one and the smaller pieces (a shelf, crates, a chest) the other. There are no rows of racks down the middle.

## Density and feel

Small and orderly, with a third to four fifths of the floor open. Spare, not stocked.

## Variants

Each culture furnishes its armoury with its own chests, crates and walls. Some are barer than others: a rack or two and a barrel, or hung swords.

## Kin rooms

- Guardrooms: small rooms round a table with two or three chairs, often one fallen; a chest; barrels in a corner; sometimes cots and straw; arms or a trophy hung on the wall; now and then a rack or two.
- Barracks: beds of one kind along a wall, a table with chairs, chests, sometimes a rack or two.

## What a designer's hand looks like here

Racks of different kinds side by side; a chest pushed in beside a pair of racks; a lone barrel. Pieces sit against the nearest wall rather than evenly spaced, and nothing is symmetric.
