# Hall: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign rooms of this type, described by their qualities, not room by room.

## What it is for

A hall of state or passage with no seat to face: an antechamber, an outer hall.

## What you notice first

The open floor and one or two kinds of piece repeated: statues, columns or obelisks, or a few things hung on the walls. Some halls hold many of one piece; some are almost empty.

## What it holds

- Common: statues or obelisks; columns; hangings (shields).
- Sometimes: a chest.
- Not seen: tables, chairs, benches, plants, rugs, shelves, stores, beds.

Each hall holds only two to four kinds of thing.

## How it is arranged

The middle is open. Either the pieces keep to the walls, one long wall often mirroring the other, statues and columns in long lines; or they stand out from the walls in a symmetric pattern with the centre left clear. The way between the doors runs clear down the middle.

## Density and feel

Open and stately: from about seven tenths open to almost all bare floor.

## Variants

Each culture brings its own statues, columns, obelisks and hangings.

## Kin rooms

- Throne rooms: a throne against a back wall facing a long open floor; statues, columns or obelisks, tapestries.
- Shrines: obelisks round one holy thing. Galleries: paintings, tapestries, plants and exhibits. Chapels: rows of benches, columns, tapestries. Great halls: tables, benches or chairs, hearths, hangings.

## What a designer's hand looks like here

Westwood repeats one piece freely in a hall in strict symmetry, or leaves a hall nearly bare with a few pieces on one wall. Variety is low; character comes from the walls and the scale of the open floor.
