# Throne room: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign rooms of this type, described by their qualities, not room by room.

Westwood's throne rooms belong to its rulers' strongholds, not to its towns. They range from a niche to a vast hall.

## What it is for

Where a ruler sits in state and is approached across a long floor.

## What you notice first

The throne, against a back wall, facing down the room toward the doors, built of several pieces (a base, a high back). Then the long, empty floor before it.

## What it holds

- Common: the throne; ceremonial pieces round it in pairs (statues, columns, obelisks); hangings repeated round the walls, sometimes many of one kind.
- Sometimes: lights in pairs along the way to the throne; odd ritual pieces set for effect; a chest or two; a tombstone.
- Not seen: tables, chairs, benches, plants, beds, shelves, stores, hunting trophies.

## How it is arranged

The long floor before the throne is left open. In some, everything stands at the walls, columns lining the side walls, and the room is symmetric about the throne. Others are looser: some pieces stand out on the floor (lights along the approach, a few tall stones), and the arrangement is not mirrored.

## Density and feel

Among the emptiest rooms in the campaign: four fifths to over nine tenths open floor, five to eight kinds of thing. Processional and vast.

## Kin rooms

Halls: open rooms with one or two kinds of piece (statues, obelisks, columns) along the walls or in a pattern, or a few hangings and statues on the back walls.

## What a designer's hand looks like here

Huge rooms left almost bare; a throne built of several pieces; one kind of hanging repeated many times round the walls; odd ritual pieces set for effect. Where Westwood repeats a piece it is a hanging, not a crowd of furniture.
