# Kitchen: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign rooms of this type, described by their qualities, not room by room.

## What it is for

Where food is cooked.

## What you notice first

The iron stove. Every one of Westwood's kitchens has one, usually on a back wall (one of the two upper walls in the picture). A bigger kitchen may have more than one fire (stoves, a wall fireplace) over several walls.

## What it holds

- Always: the iron stove; a table, which may be long, square, oval or round with food on it.
- Sometimes: a chair or two; barrels, a few or many, along the walls; a picture on the wall.
- Absent: cauldrons, sacks, apple crates, shelves of provisions, crates, food on the floor, plants and ornaments. These are working rooms with few kinds of thing, about four.

## How it is arranged

The stoves stand against the walls, and where there are several they are spread over several walls rather than in one line. The tables go along a front wall or a little out from it, toward the middle. Barrels stand along the back walls and a side wall in loose groups. The middle is mostly clear, and the doors (often two or three, since a kitchen is passed through) open onto open floor.

## Density and feel

Plain, open and practical. A small kitchen can be almost bare: a stove, a table, a chair. A big one is busy with fires, long tables and barrels, and still about half of its floor is open.

## Variants

Small household kitchens are spare; a castle's is large, with long tables, many barrels and more than one fire.

## What a designer's hand looks like here

The stove sits wherever the room allows, not centred. Tables of different shapes share one room, a picture may hang over a bare kitchen, and barrels are grouped where they would be used rather than spaced evenly.
