# Westwood's rooms: what every real one is like

For the blind judge: what Westwood's campaign rooms (Con, War, Wiz) have in common, whatever their type. Read this,
then the type's own description, `review/roomlab/judging/<type>.md`. These describe Westwood's rooms as they are; they
are not anyone's rules.

## Reading the picture

The camera looks down from the south. The two upper walls of a room in the picture (top right and top left) are its
back walls: you see their faces and whatever stands against them from the front. The two lower walls are its front
walls: they are drawn half see-through, and what stands against them is seen from behind. Westwood mostly puts the
pieces with a face (shelves, bookcases, hearths, stoves, desks, chests, hangings) on the back walls.

## Purpose

A real room reads at once as its type and as a place someone uses: one purpose, one theme, the furnishing of one
household or garrison. Its key piece (a bed, a bar, a hearth, a throne, an altar, a forge) is there and placed where
it makes sense, and the other pieces gather round the use.

## Walls and the middle

Most of Westwood's rooms put nearly everything against two or three walls and leave the middle open. Pieces stand
square and close to their wall, the right way round. Pieces made to repeat (bookcases, shelves, workstations, pews,
beds in a bunk room, barrels in a store) stand in runs or heaps; single pieces (a desk, a cauldron, a crystal ball)
stand once. A table group or a carpet may hold the middle.

## Spacing and grouping

Things gather in groups with open floor between them: a desk with its chair and a bookcase beside it, a table with its
chairs, barrels in a knot with a crate pushed in. Westwood's rooms rarely space single pieces evenly across the floor,
and rarely jam pieces into each other. There is a clear way in from each door, and the floor in front of a hearth,
stove or chest is kept clear.

## Variety and density

A believable mix for the type, a handful to a dozen kinds of object, rather than one kind repeated across the room.
Private rooms are small and full, halls open and ordered, stores crammed, cells and crypts bare.

## A designer's hand

Westwood's rooms have small irregularities with intent: a chair pulled out or knocked over, a rug a little off, one odd
lived-in piece (a barrel by a bed, a spittoon by a desk), a room shaped round an alcove or a niche, a carpet laid in the
floor to the room's shape, sister rooms that share a layout but differ by a piece. Symmetry where it matters (a throne,
an altar, a hall), not everywhere. What reads as false is mechanical regularity (every piece in a row, the same gap
everywhere, the same set stamped again and again) or randomness without intent (pieces scattered, facing oddly, a light
in a corner for no reason).
