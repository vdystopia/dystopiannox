# Cell: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign rooms of this type, described by their qualities, not room by room.

## What it is for

A room someone is locked in. Westwood's are of two kinds: small gaol cells, and larger pens where a brutes' camp keeps its captives.

## What you notice first

In a gaol cell, the cot against a back wall. In a pen, the heaps of straw on a bare floor. Each opens through a single barred jail door.

## What it holds

- Gaol cells (very small): a cot and heaped bundles of straw, and nothing else; some hold only the cot.
- Pens (larger): a handful of heaps of crude straw, often with one rough upright piece standing free in the floor (stocks or a post). A pen sometimes has a single stool, a rough bench or a barrel.
- Sometimes: bars dividing a larger cell, remains lying in the straw, a barrel or two in a corner.
- Not seen: tables, chairs (beyond a stool), chests, shelves, hangings, rugs, plants, statues, hearths. Cells have no lights of their own.

## How it is arranged

In a gaol cell the cot is against the wall farthest from the door, and the straw is heaped along one or two walls beside it. In the pens almost nothing touches the walls: the straw lies in loose heaps out in the floor, with the upright piece among them. The doorway is kept clear.

## Density and feel

Bare and hard. A gaol cell is small and fairly full, with the straw taking up much of it. A pen is mostly empty floor, about two thirds open.

## Variants

The gaol kind is cut stone and cobbles behind a jail door; the pen kind rough stone, crude straw and cobwebs.

## What a designer's hand looks like here

- Straw heaped unevenly and banked against one wall, not spread in an even carpet.
- Cells side by side that differ: one has straw, its neighbour has only the cot.
- A pen with one stool or one barrel, as if left behind.
- Remains lying in the straw.
