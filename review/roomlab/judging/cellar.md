# Cellar: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign rooms of this type, described by their qualities, not room by room.

## What it is for

A keg cellar: the ale, wine and water of a house or keep, kept in barrels.

## What you notice first

Kegs, and little else: in knots in the corners, or crowded together in one tight block. A bigger cellar may have a larger cask standing out from the walls with kegs about it.

## What it holds

- Kegs in every one, a handful to under a dozen.
- Sometimes: a few dark crates by the door or beside the kegs; a larger cask; a stack of piled barrels; a floor lever.
- Not in Westwood's cellars: shelves, sacks, apple crates, tables, chairs, racks or statues; they have no light inside, or one at most.

## How it is arranged

The kegs gather in knots against the walls, mostly in the corners, sometimes two or three in a short run, with some standing a step out into the room. The floor between the knots is left open; a small cellar may be half bare. The way in from the door is clear.

## Density and feel

Small and plain, with one to five kinds of thing. A cellar with few kegs is mostly open floor; a packed one leaves only about a third of its floor open.

## Variants

Cellars of different cultures look much alike: plain store rooms of kegs, with the walls and floor telling the culture.

## What a designer's hand looks like here

Unevenness: the kegs in two knots of different sizes rather than one even row, a crate or two pushed against the kegs, a big piece standing out on its own. Keeps may share one cellar layout, so a plain, near-empty cellar is as real as a full one.

## Kin rooms

Westwood's storerooms mix their stock: kegs, crates, apple crates, water barrels, shelves of provisions, a chest, sometimes a table and chair or a fallen chair; a working store may hold steel crates, tool barrels and tools. A powder store is black-powder kegs in knots and short rows, almost nothing else, left dark.
