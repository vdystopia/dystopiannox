# Pond with a dock: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described by their qualities, not scene by scene.

**What it is for.** A landing on the water where boats tie up, with the fishers' or traders' goods kept beside it.

**What you notice first.** A long wooden pier running straight out from the bank into open water, along one of the diagonal lines, with water on both sides and around its end.

**What it holds.** The pier itself is a ramp at one end, plank sections, and an end piece; most of Westwood's are long runs of this kind, some shorter. Pieces seen sometimes are a few barrels, a crate, rock pillars, and a boulder or two on the bank. Some docks carry nothing at all. Westwood's docks have no nets, boats, benches or fishing tackle.

**How it is laid out.** The pier is the whole scene, a single straight line from land to water that is long and thin. Any goods stand in a small knot of two or three barrels, at one end of the pier. A crate may sit alone a little apart. Rocks sit on the bank nearby. The pier points out toward the middle of the water and never runs along the bank.

**Density and feel.** Very sparse: four to ten pieces, most of them the pier itself. It feels calm and purposeful.

**Settings.** Town shores on wide water; a shore may hold more than one dock.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** Docks that share a shore differ in what they carry: bare, a few barrels, a lone crate. Rocks on the bank break up the shore. The pier's length suits the breadth of the water it reaches into.
