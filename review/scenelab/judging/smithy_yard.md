# Smithy yard: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described from the scenes themselves. Westwood has only one outdoor smithy yard, plus an anvil and bellows inside one war camp, so the real pictures will be few or none.

**What it is for.** The smith's working yard, where metal is forged and the finished work is laid out for sale.

**What you notice first.** An anvil, with a bin of cinders and a pair of water barrels for quenching close by.

**What it holds.** Westwood's one smithy yard, in a castle town, has the anvil, the cinder bin, two water barrels, a pair of trader's counters, two armour racks, a clothes rack, two torch poles and an ornate street lamp. In the war camp, the anvil and bellows stand beside a bow rack and polearm racks. Westwood's smithies have no piles of ore, stacked ingots, grindstones or heaps of scrap.

**How it is laid out.** The yard stands against the town's walls, with more than half of its pieces close to a wall. The work corner is a tight group of anvil, cinder bin and water barrels. The counters stand a little apart in a short line, and the armour and clothes racks are spaced out beyond them, like a display. Torch poles and the street lamp mark the edges. The pieces are loose, with walking room between them, not crammed.

**Density and feel.** About a dozen pieces of eight kinds, spread over a fair-sized yard with a little open ground. It feels like a working shop that also sells its wares.

**Settings.** A castle town's lanes, set back from the main road against built walls. The camp version is in the open on a town map.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** The work and the sale are kept in separate places: forge things together in one spot, display things spread along a wall. Light is placed for working after dark. The yard is fitted between buildings rather than set in a field.
