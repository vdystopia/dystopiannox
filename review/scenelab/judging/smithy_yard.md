# Smithy yard: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described by their qualities, not scene by scene. Westwood has almost no outdoor smithy yards, so the real pictures will be few or none.

**What it is for.** The smith's working yard, where metal is forged and the finished work is laid out for sale.

**What you notice first.** An anvil, with a bin of cinders and water barrels for quenching close by.

**What it holds.** The forge pieces (anvil, cinder bin or bellows, water barrels), and where the work is sold, counters and racks of armour, clothes or arms; torch poles or a lamp for working after dark. Westwood's smithies have no piles of ore, stacked ingots, grindstones or heaps of scrap.

**How it is laid out.** The yard stands against built walls, with most of its pieces close to a wall. The work corner is a tight group of anvil, cinder bin and water barrels. Any counters stand a little apart in a short line, and racks are spaced out beyond them, like a display. Lights mark the edges. The pieces are loose, with walking room between them, not crammed.

**Density and feel.** About a dozen pieces of several kinds, spread over a fair-sized yard with a little open ground. It feels like a working shop that also sells its wares.

**Settings.** Town lanes, set back from the main road against built walls; now and then a smith's corner within a camp.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** The work and the sale are kept in separate places: forge things together in one spot, display things spread along a wall. Light is placed for working after dark. The yard is fitted between buildings rather than set in a field.
