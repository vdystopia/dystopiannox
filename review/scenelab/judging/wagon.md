# Wagon: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described from the scenes themselves.

**What it is for.** A cart left where it was used, either parked with its load or broken down and abandoned.

**What you notice first.** In Westwood the cart is nearly always a small mine ore cart. It is either whole and parked, or broken and tipped over with a wheel lying off to one side.

**What it holds.** Common pieces are the ore cart (sometimes two side by side), small and medium stones, and barrels. Pieces seen sometimes are the loose wheel, a barrel of tools, an iron barrel, a crate, a signpost, a rock pillar, and a cobweb in a corner. The few large travelling carts in Westwood's campaign appear inside camps and at market stalls, not as scenes of their own. Westwood's wagon scenes have no horses' gear, sacks of grain or spilled cargo fanned across the road.

**How it is laid out.** The cart stands close against a wall, fence or rock face, with its back to something solid. Its few companions stay within a few steps: a barrel or two, a tool barrel, a handful of rubble stones. Broken carts sit with their wheel beside them and a few stones about. One broken cart has a scatter of barrels around it, some in a loose line along the wall. A signpost nearby suggests a junction or the way to a mine.

**Density and feel.** Small and tight. Three to ten pieces, with only three or four kinds. It feels left behind, not staged.

**Settings.** Every Westwood wagon is in a town map, by mine workings and along town lanes, usually near a path but not on it.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** The cart is tucked against a wall, not left in the open. The few things around it relate to it, such as tools, rubble or the lost wheel. A sign tells where the road goes.
