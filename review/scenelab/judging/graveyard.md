# Graveyard: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described from the scenes themselves.

**What it is for.** Where a town buries its dead: graves marked by headstones, sometimes a crypt or monument for the important dead.

**What you notice first.** Headstones, and little else. In most of Westwood's graveyards they make up nearly every piece in view, standing in loose rows on open grass.

**What it holds.** Common pieces are plain headstones, usually of only one or two designs. Sometimes the scene also has a pair of stone monuments or pillars at one edge like gateposts, readable headstones, a stone crypt or sarcophagus, a wall torch, a few barren dead shrubs, or a row of gargoyle statues in a grand castle cemetery. Westwood's graveyards do not have benches, open graves, coffins, spades, tool buckets, urns or strewn bones.

**How it is laid out.** The headstones step along the diagonal wall lines in rough rows. They are spread wide apart, with plenty of bare grass between graves. The rows are not exact: spacing varies, a row may stop short, and a grave sits a little out of line. Where there are monuments, they stand as a pair at one edge, like gateposts. Many graveyards lie beside a path rather than behind walls. Some stand against a town or castle wall or a fence, and a few small ones are just three or four stones tucked by a wall.

**Density and feel.** Sparse and quiet. Typically under a dozen pieces, sometimes twenty or more in a large yard, with more than half of the ground left open.

**Settings.** Mostly towns. Some are in castle grounds, one in a forest and one in a swamp.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** Restraint. A single kind of stone, set out with care along the grid's diagonals but never perfectly, with open ground left to breathe. A pair of monuments marks one edge, and grander yards add statues or a crypt.
