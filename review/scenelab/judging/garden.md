# Vegetable garden: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described by their qualities, not scene by scene.

**What it is for.** Food grown by a household or a village: crops in beds, watered and gathered.

**What you notice first.** A dense patch of crops, planted thickly in rows that run along the diagonal wall lines.

**What it holds.** Two or three kinds of crop is normal (corn, tomatoes, cabbages). Pieces seen sometimes are water barrels, barrels and crates of apples set at the garden's edge, a torch pole, a hollow stump, and small stones; loose stones may lie among the rows of a large field. Westwood's gardens have no spades, tool racks or cages.

**How it is laid out.** Crops are grouped in bands of one kind: a strip of one crop next to a strip of another. Within a band the plants stand close together in diagonal lines, and the bands lie side by side, sometimes slightly overlapping at their ends. The barrels and crates gather in small knots at the corners and edges, not among the plants. A garden fits its spot: along a path or a fence, against a building's wall, or spread across open ground with its stores at the ends.

**Density and feel.** Crowded inside, with hardly any open ground within the beds. Sizes vary a lot, from a small plot to a field of many dozens of plants. It looks tended and productive.

**Settings.** Villages, farmland and grounds, beside the houses that tend them.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** Bands that are uneven in length and break off at a ragged edge, and rows that drift slightly. Stores sit where a worker would leave them. The garden is sized and shaped to fit its spot beside a house, path or fence.
