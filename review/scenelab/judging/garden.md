# Vegetable garden: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described from the scenes themselves.

**What it is for.** Food grown by a household or a village: crops in beds, watered and gathered.

**What you notice first.** A dense patch of crops (corn, tomatoes and cabbages), planted thickly in rows that run along the diagonal wall lines.

**What it holds.** Every Westwood garden has corn and tomatoes, and most have cabbages too, so two or three crops is normal. Pieces seen sometimes are water barrels, barrels and crates of apples set at the garden's edge, a torch pole, a hollow stump, and small stones. In the largest field, loose bricks or stones lie scattered among the rows. Westwood's gardens have no spades, tool racks or cages.

**How it is laid out.** Crops are grouped in bands of one kind: a strip of corn next to a strip of tomatoes next to a strip of cabbages. Within a band the plants stand close together in diagonal lines, and the bands lie side by side, sometimes slightly overlapping at their ends. The barrels and crates gather in small knots at the corners and edges, not among the plants. One garden runs along a path beside a fence, another lies partly against a building's wall. The large village field spreads across open ground with its stores at either end.

**Density and feel.** Crowded inside, with hardly any open ground within the beds. Sizes vary a lot: a small plot of about a dozen plants, a household garden of a few dozen, or a field of a hundred pieces. It looks tended and productive.

**Settings.** Forest villages, a town's farmland, a castle's grounds and a swamp village.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** Bands that are uneven in length and break off at a ragged edge, and rows that drift slightly. Stores sit where a worker would leave them. The garden is sized and shaped to fit its spot beside a house, path or fence.
