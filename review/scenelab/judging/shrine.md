# Shrine or waystone: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described from the scenes themselves.

**What it is for.** A place of reverence or remembrance, such as a statue, a memorial, a tomb or a waymark, set where it will be seen.

**What you notice first.** A carved statue (or two or three) standing against a wall, usually with a torch on the wall beside it or a torch pole close by.

**What it holds.** Common pieces are statues of a few designs, wall torches and torch poles, stone monuments, and in the forest a stone tomb or sarcophagus. Pieces seen sometimes are a cross, a small chest or offering box, carved milestones and low altars by a road with a signpost, tapestries, columns, a candelabrum and benches in a castle chapel, a lit flame basin, and cobwebs. Westwood's shrines have no flowers laid out, no ring of offerings and no fenced enclosure.

**How it is laid out.** Nearly all of them stand right against a wall: a castle or town wall, or the stonework of a ruin. A common arrangement is a line of statues along a wall with torches between them, alternating statue and light at an even beat. Elsewhere two designs of statue alternate down a line with a monument at its head. In the forest ruins, tombs and monuments stand in short lines with a cross or torch poles between them. By the road, milestones and altars step along beside it in a line, with the sign at one end.

**Density and feel.** Small and formal: two to eight pieces typically, rarely over a dozen, of one to a few kinds. It feels deliberate and ceremonial.

**Settings.** Forest ruins, castle walls and courts, and town roadsides.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** Symmetry and rhythm: pairs, alternations, lines of statues, lights set where they show the statue off. The wall behind is part of the design, and a statue standing alone in a field is not Westwood's way.
