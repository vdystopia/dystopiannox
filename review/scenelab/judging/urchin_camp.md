# Urchin camp: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described by their qualities, not scene by scene.

**What it is for.** A squatters' den: a hollow in the rock furnished from scavenged junk for sleeping, eating around a table and hoarding pickings.

**What you notice first.** Ramshackle furniture lining a rock wall. There are low beds or hammocks, crude shelves crammed with stuff, and pictures and scrolls hung on the stone.

**What it holds.** Common pieces are scavenged beds (or hammocks, or flat pallets), full shelves of several makes (plank, log and tepee-shaped), stools, hung paintings and scrolls, barrels, wall torches and torch poles, and small chests. Pieces seen sometimes are a big table ringed by stools, empty shelves, a small table, nightstands, pelts, boulders, and a litter of straw. Urchin dens have no tents, fires, racks of arms or carts.

**How it is laid out.** Almost everything hugs the rock wall. Shelves, paintings, beds and chests line the edge of the hollow, and the middle stays fairly open. When there is a table, it stands in the open with three to five stools packed close around it. Beds of one kind are set side by side in a row, and some dens are nothing but a few beds. Barrels sit in small groups in corners.

**Density and feel.** Busy and cluttered at the edges, with eight or so kinds and around twenty pieces typical (from a handful up to several dozen). It looks lived-in and makeshift.

**Settings.** Pockets and tunnels of natural rock with dirt floors, under and beside towns, and some in the wild. They lie far from any road, and dens come in warrens of many hollows near one another.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** Each hollow has its own character: a sleeping den, a dining den or a store of pickings. Furniture follows the curve of the rock. The mix of kinds of shelf and bed makes the stuff look scavenged, yet things of one use stay together.
