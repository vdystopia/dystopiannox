# Market stall: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described by their qualities, not scene by scene. Westwood has few of them.

**What it is for.** A trader's pitch: a striped awning to sell under, with the stock set out around it.

**What you notice first.** A large two-colour striped awning on its poles.

**What it holds.** Common pieces are the awning and its poles, barrels and water barrels, and iron-bound crates. Pieces seen sometimes are crates of food, a cart, an armour rack, torch poles, and the tools of the trade, so what is sold shows in what stands there. A stump or a rock may sit at the edge. Westwood's stalls have no tables of goods, signs or crowds of baskets.

**How it is laid out.** The awning sits in the middle. The stock gathers around it in small clusters: barrels in a short row of three or four, a pair of crates, a crate on its own. A cart is parked at the side. A torch pole or two may stand at the edges. Each stall's goods fit its trade.

**Density and feel.** Compact and full. A dozen or so pieces close together, with hardly any open ground inside the pitch. Half a dozen kinds is typical.

**Settings.** Towns and settlements, often right beside a road.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** The stock tells you what is sold. Barrels are lined up as if just unloaded and the cart is left beside them. Westwood's stalls differ from one another in colour and in goods.
