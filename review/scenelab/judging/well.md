# Well: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described by their qualities, not scene by scene. Westwood has few of them.

**What it is for.** The well where water is fetched, a landmark in a town square or a castle court.

**What you notice first.** The well itself, a round stone wishing well, standing more or less alone.

**What it holds.** The ornate wishing well, often with a signpost nearby. Another scene (a trader's pitch, say) may stand near it, but as a neighbour, not as the well's own furnishing. Westwood's wells have no barrels, buckets, benches, troughs or water carts beside them.

**How it is laid out.** The well stands on open ground near a road, a short way off the road and not on it. A signpost, when there is one, stands a little apart and does not touch the well. Nothing crowds the well's own footprint.

**Density and feel.** Nearly empty. One or two pieces is usual. The well reads as a public landmark that is clear on all sides so it can be walked up to.

**Settings.** Courtyards, squares and village greens.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** Restraint and placement. The well is set near a road, is given breathing room, and has at most a sign to tell travellers where they are. Westwood does not dress its wells, so a well surrounded by props is unlike any of Westwood's.
