# Well: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described from the scenes themselves. Westwood has only four.

**What it is for.** The well where water is fetched, a landmark in a town square or a castle court.

**What you notice first.** The well itself, a round stone wishing well, standing more or less alone.

**What it holds.** Every Westwood well is the same ornate wishing well. Often a signpost stands nearby. Once, in a castle market, it shares its corner with a trader's armour racks, helmet shelves, a clothes rack, crossed weapons on display and an ornate street lamp. Westwood's wells have no barrels, buckets, benches, troughs or water carts beside them.

**How it is laid out.** The well stands on open ground near a road, a short way off the road and not on it. A signpost, when there is one, stands a little apart and does not touch the well. In the one busier example, the well sits at a corner of a trader's pitch, and the racks line up along a wall a short distance away, so the well is a neighbour of that scene rather than part of it. Nothing crowds the well's own footprint.

**Density and feel.** Nearly empty. One or two pieces is usual. The well reads as a public landmark that is clear on all sides so it can be walked up to.

**Settings.** Castle courtyards, a town square and a swamp village. The same layout is reused across the three campaigns' versions of a town.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** Restraint and placement. The well is set near a road, is given breathing room, and has at most a sign to tell travellers where they are. Westwood does not dress its wells, so a well surrounded by props is unlike any of Westwood's.
