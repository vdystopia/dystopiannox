# Quarry: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described by their qualities, not scene by scene. Westwood has very few, and they differ widely, so the real pictures will be few.

**What it is for.** Where stone or ore is dug: the tools of the work, the timber that shores up the face, and the stores of the workings.

**What you notice first.** Mining gear set against a built or rock wall: a timber post, tools stood by the face or driven into the ground, rock nearby.

**What it holds.** Mining gear (a mine post, a set of tools, a shovel), barrels, rocks and boulders; a larger working may add crates, chests, wall torches, machine parts and the odd piece of a living camp. Westwood's quarries have no heaps of cut stone blocks, carts of ore, cranes or scaffolding.

**How it is laid out.** They cling to walls, with almost every piece close against one. Barrels form a tight little group beside the tools; in a larger working the furnishings line the walls in a loose band, chests and crates in small groups, with the rock set off at one end. Little ground is left open.

**Density and feel.** From a tiny scene of a few pieces of three kinds to a larger one of a dozen kinds.

**Settings.** Away from roads, at a town's edge or tucked into the wild.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** The work is told through a few telling tools set against the face, rather than a field of rubble. Westwood's examples are sparse and wall-hugging, and a quarry laid out as an open pit full of rocks has no counterpart among them.
