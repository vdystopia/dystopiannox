# Quarry: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described from the scenes themselves. Westwood has only two, and they differ widely, so the real pictures will be few.

**What it is for.** Where stone or ore is dug: the tools of the work, the timber that shores up the face, and the stores of the workings.

**What you notice first.** Mining gear set against a built or rock wall. In one, a timber mine post and a bundle of mining tools stand by the wall. In the other, a shovel is driven into the ground among boulders.

**What it holds.** The smaller quarry, on a town map, has a mine post, a set of mining tools and three barrels, all against a wall. The larger, in a forest, has a shovel in the ground, a boulder and a heap of rocks, iron-bound crates, open chests, wall torches, a gear wheel, a fallen chair, hung tapestries, a workbench and an alchemist's desk. Neither has heaps of cut stone blocks, carts of ore, cranes or scaffolding.

**How it is laid out.** Both cling to walls, with almost every piece close against one. In the small quarry the barrels form a tight little triangle beside the post and tools. In the larger one the furnishings line the walls in a loose band, chests and crates in small groups, with the boulders and the gear set off at the far end. Little ground is left open in either.

**Density and feel.** One is tiny, five pieces of three kinds. The other is a dozen kinds over about twenty pieces.

**Settings.** A town's edge and a forest hideaway, both away from roads.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** The work is told through a few telling tools set against the face, rather than a field of rubble. Westwood's examples are sparse and wall-hugging, and any quarry picture laid out as an open pit full of rocks has no counterpart among them.
