# Jail yard: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described from the scenes themselves.

**What it is for.** The town's or castle's cells, small lock-ups behind barred doors, sometimes with the jailer's room of arms beside them.

**What you notice first.** Barred cell doors set into stone walls, each with a torch on the wall beside it.

**What it holds.** Common pieces are wall torches by the doors, and inside the cells straw strewn on the floor and a simple cot. Pieces seen sometimes are a water barrel and a table, and on the walls of a guardroom next to the cells, hung swords, a hung crossbow, a bow rack, a quiver rack and a polearm. One cell in a castle has a candelabrum. Westwood's jails have no stocks, gallows, chains, buckets or open courtyards dressed with props.

**How it is laid out.** Cells come in a row along a wall, each door with its own torch. Several cells are completely bare, with only the torch outside. Where a cell is furnished, the cot stands against the back or side wall and the straw is spread in a loose drift across the floor, never neatly. Next to the cells, the guardroom keeps its weapons hung along the walls, with the table in the room and the water barrel in a corner.

**Density and feel.** Usually very bare: one to three pieces per cell. A full jail with its guardroom reaches about twenty pieces, half of them straw. Everything is pressed against built walls, and it feels cramped, grim and functional.

**Settings.** Mostly castles, with several cells in a row, and a few towns, each with a single jail and guardroom. Some lie beside a road.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** Consistency along the row of doors and torches, with variety inside: one cell bare, one with a cot, one deep in straw. Arms hang on the guardroom walls rather than standing in the open.
