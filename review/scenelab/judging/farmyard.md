# Farmyard: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign scenes of this type, described from the scenes themselves.

**What it is for.** Fodder and bedding kept on a farmstead or in a work yard: heaps of straw and the barrels that go with them.

**What you notice first.** Heaps of straw piled loosely against a wall or a rock face.

**What it holds.** Every Westwood farmyard has straw heaps and a barrel or two. Common extras are a torch on the wall, boulders, and rock pillars. Seen once each are a stack of piled barrels and the broken pieces of an iron fence lying in the straw. Westwood's farmyards have no sacks of feed, hay carts, windmills, troughs, pitchforks or threshing floors.

**How it is laid out.** The straw lies in a loose drift of three to ten heaps, some touching, some a step apart, along the foot of a wall or cliff. Barrels stand apart from the straw, in a pair or alone at one end. The torch is fixed to the wall above. In the biggest yard, straw heaps sweep along a curve of rock with stones and boulders at the far end. In another, the straw is half buried among wrecked iron fencing, like a pen that has fallen in.

**Density and feel.** Sparse to modest. Five to about twenty pieces of two to six kinds, with straw nearly always the commonest. It feels like a corner of rough working land: practical, a bit untidy, and lived against its wall.

**Settings.** Forest settlements and town outskirts, set back from the roads, often in a nook of rock or against a building.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** The straw follows the wall or rock it is heaped against, not a grid. Barrels are kept a little apart from it. Small story touches such as a collapsed fence or a stack of barrels make a yard feel used. Westwood keeps these scenes simple, with no farm machinery.
