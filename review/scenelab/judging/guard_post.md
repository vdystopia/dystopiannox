# Guard post: what a real one is like

For the blind judge: Westwood's campaign has almost no outdoor guard post, so this page describes the scene from its purpose and says what little Westwood shows. The real pictures may be few or none.

**What it is for.** A lit watch-point at a gate, a bridge or the edge of a camp, from which a way in is watched at night.

**What you notice first.** For a post like this, the anchor would be a light: a standing brazier or a torch pole by the way it watches.

**What it holds.** Westwood's only scene of this kind is not a sentry post at all. It is a single brazier standing in a castle workplace crowded with wizard's workbenches, desks, dark wooden chairs, crystal balls, a large electrical apparatus, an orb and two lanterns. Outdoors, the lights at the edge of Westwood's camps are torch poles, never a brazier set up as a post with a seat, rack and water barrel.

**How it is laid out.** In that one castle scene, workbenches form long rows with the other pieces packed in among them, and the brazier stands at the edge of the group. At camps, Westwood's torch poles stand singly at the edge of the place.

**Density and feel.** The castle scene is dense, about thirty pieces, mostly a single kind of workbench. A real outdoor watch-point, as Westwood treats it, is minimal: one light and little else.

**Settings.** The one match is in a castle's grounds. Lights at the edges of camps occur in towns, forests and swamps.

**What a designer's hand looks like here.** Westwood lets one light do the job of marking a watched way. If a picture shows an elaborate furnished post, it is unlike anything in Westwood's campaign. If it shows a brazier among benches of work, it may be Westwood's own odd case.
